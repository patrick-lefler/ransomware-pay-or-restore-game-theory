A Ransomware Bargaining Game for the Board: Summary

Author

Patrick Lefler

Published

November 19, 2026

All money is in $M and all values are illustrative. The firm is synthetic.

Introduction

“If we’re hit, should we pay, and how do we decide before it happens?”

This site works through that question for a 500-person fintech software firm, the same firm as our Defender–Attacker study. A ransomware crew has encrypted the firm’s production platform and copied its client data. The firm must choose between paying the crew’s demand and restoring from backups. We model that choice as a bargaining game in which the crew does not know how badly we need to pay, and learns from what we do.

The answer, in three sentences:

  1. Every firm has a walk-away point: the largest ransom worth paying, after allowing for a decryptor that may fail, sanctions exposure, the costs of paying, and the leak risk that remains either way.
  2. That point is set by how long it takes to restore; the size of the demand only tests it. Each day cut from restore time lowers it by about $0.31M.
  3. Resilience therefore does two jobs: it lowers the cost of an outage, and it makes a “we don’t pay” policy credible, so crews that learn over repeated incidents attack less often.

How to read the plot. Restore time runs across the bottom and the crew’s demand runs up the side. Each line is our walk-away point at one level of backup credibility. A demand below the line is worth paying; a demand above it is not. The shaded areas show the pay (amber) and don’t-pay (blue) regions for our own backups. The black dot is our firm today: a 10-day restore and a $2.5M demand.

What the plot tells you. Restore time sets where the line sits; the demand only decides which side of it we fall. At the most likely values our firm sits just above its line, with a walk-away point of $2.39M. Allowing for uncertainty in every input, paying a $2.5M demand on day one is cheaper in 64% of simulated incidents: with today’s restore times, this firm is often a rational payer. Weak backups raise the line to $4.32M at a 10-day restore.

Three findings

Restore time sets the threshold. The walk-away point does not depend on the demand at all: restore time sets it, and the demand only tests it. Across 200,000 simulated incidents, expected outage time (restore time, backup credibility and rebuild time combined) explains 69% of the variation in the walk-away point. When demands vary over a moderate range, it also explains more of the pay decision (39%) than the demand does (15%); if demands vary as widely as published payments, the demand comes first (sensitivity table). Cutting restore from 10 to 5 days lowers the walk-away point from $2.39M to $0.84M.

Paying is a poor first move. The decryptor may fail, decryption itself takes days, the stolen data may leak anyway, a sanctions link can surface after payment, and regulators must be told either way. At the most likely values, paying a $2.5M demand on day one costs $10.83M in expectation, against $10.70M for restoring: within about 1% of each other, so the answer depends on the inputs. Restoring first and paying only if backups fail costs $9.23M, less than either.

A credible refusal means fewer attacks, and credibility requires resilience. Crews learn from what we do, and a crew that expects less attacks less often. Over 10 years at a 15% annual attack rate, never paying costs our firm $13.2M against $15.6M for paying case by case. The test comes at the first incident, when a fragile firm with a 21-day restore would save $4.8M by paying. In the model, the extra attacks that one payment invites cost it more (present value $16.2M against $14.0M), so the policy holds, but only if crews favor firms that have paid before. Without that, paying once is cheaper, and only resilience removes the temptation.

## Three findings

**Restore time sets the threshold.** The walk-away point does not depend on the demand at all: restore time sets it, and the demand only tests it. Across `r fmt_num(N_MC, 0)` simulated incidents, expected outage time (restore time, backup credibility and rebuild time combined) explains `r fmt_pct(share_of("R_star", "E_T"))` of the variation in the walk-away point. When demands vary over a moderate range, it also explains more of the pay decision (`r fmt_pct(share_of("pay", "E_T"))`) than the demand does (`r fmt_pct(share_of("pay", "D"))`); if demands vary as widely as published payments, the demand comes first ([sensitivity table](governance.qmd#sensitivity)). Cutting restore from 10 to 5 days lowers the walk-away point from `r fmt_m(wa$R_base, 2)` to `r fmt_m(R_of("Fast restore"), 2)`.

**Paying is a poor first move.** The decryptor may fail, decryption itself takes days, the stolen data may leak anyway, a sanctions link can surface after payment, and regulators must be told either way. At the most likely values, paying a `r fmt_m(D_BASE)` demand on day one costs `r fmt_m(wa$C_pay, 2)` in expectation, against `r fmt_m(wa$C_refuse, 2)` for restoring: within about `r fmt_pct(abs(wa$C_pay / wa$C_refuse - 1))` of each other, so the answer depends on the inputs. Restoring first and paying only if backups fail costs `r fmt_m(wa$C_staged, 2)`, less than either.

**A credible refusal means fewer attacks, and credibility requires resilience.** Crews learn from what we do, and a crew that expects less attacks less often. Over 10 years at a `r fmt_pct(P$reputation$p0_base)` annual attack rate, never paying costs our firm `r fmt_m(cost_of("typical", "never"))` against `r fmt_m(cost_of("typical", "rational"))` for paying case by case. The test comes at the first incident, when a fragile firm with a 21-day restore would save `r fmt_m(fr$in_moment_saving)` by paying. In the model, the extra attacks that one payment invites cost it more (present value `r fmt_m(pv_of(eta_b, "fragile", "deviate_once"))` against `r fmt_m(pv_of(eta_b, "fragile", "never"))`), so the policy holds, but only if crews favor firms that have paid before. Without that, paying once is cheaper, and only resilience removes the temptation.
