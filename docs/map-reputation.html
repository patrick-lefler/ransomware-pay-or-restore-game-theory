Weaker backups push the line up and turn more demands into rational payments. Stronger backups and faster restores pull it down. Below about 2.3 days of restore time at base credibility, no demand is worth paying.

What each choice teaches the crew

Each incident is also a lesson for the crew. A crew that is paid concludes we value our systems more than it guessed, and asks for more next time. A crew that is refused lowers its estimate, and its next demand falls. We simulate a 10-year horizon in which the crew learns from every incident and favors targets that have paid before. For a firm that never pays, lower demands are not the benefit; the benefit is that a crew expecting less attacks less often.

We compare two policies. Pay when worth it means paying whenever, in that incident, the demand is below our walk-away point. Never pay means refusing every time, committed in advance.

How to read the plot. Each row is a firm profile: resilient (4-day restore, 0.90 backup credibility), our firm (10 days, 0.80) and fragile (21 days, 0.60). Blue dots are never pay; orange dots pay when worth it. The resilient firm never pays under either policy, so it has one gray dot. Each label gives the expected 10-year cost and expected number of attacks. Drag the slider to change the annual chance of an attack.

What the plot tells you. At 15%, never paying saves our firm about $2.4M and the fragile firm about $5.9M over 10 years. The saving comes from fewer attacks: 2.2 in 10 years for the fragile firm if it pays when worth it, against 1.2 if it never pays. The saving grows quickly with the attack rate. The resilient firm would refuse every demand anyway, so for it the policy costs nothing to keep.

The more often you expect to be attacked, the more a reputation for refusing is worth. At a 5% annual rate, the fragile firm is slightly better off paying case by case (by $1.2M over 10 years), because it is rarely attacked often enough for reputation to matter. From about 9% upward, never paying is cheaper for both firms.

Why the policy must be credible

A no-pay policy is only worth what the crew believes about it, and a policy breaks under pressure when breaking it pays. In the moment, a fragile firm facing the first $2.53M demand saves about $4.8M by paying, because its walk-away point is $6.87M.

To test whether that temptation should win, we compare never paying with paying once, at the first incident, and never again. Costs are in present value at a 8% discount rate, so the saving on the day counts in full and later attacks count for less. For the fragile firm, paying once costs $16.2M over 10 years against $14.0M for never paying: the extra attacks it invites outweigh the saving, so the policy is credible, by a margin of $2.2M. For our firm the margin is $1.3M. If crews ignore past payments, the result reverses: paying once saves the fragile firm $3.2M, and the policy is not credible.

A resilient firm faces no such test. Its walk-away point is −$0.67M, so refusing is also its cheapest move on the day. Its policy is believed because it never has to be enforced against its own interest. Resilience is what turns a stated policy into a believed one.

  tab_style(cell_text(weight = "bold"),
            cells_body(columns = fragile_never, rows = fragile_never < fragile_rational)) |>

How to read the table. Each row sets how strongly crews favor firms that have paid before. Zero means they don’t care; one means a firm whose expected payout is twice as high is attacked twice as often. Each cell is the expected 10-year cost for one firm and policy, in $M. The cheaper policy in each row and firm is in bold.

What the table tells you. With no targeting response, paying when worth it is always cheaper, because it is the best choice in each incident by construction. Never paying wins once crews favor known payers: above about 0.24 for our firm and 0.59 for the fragile firm. The whole reputation benefit rests on this one behavioral assumption. If crews ignore past payments, the case for never paying must rest on other grounds, such as sanctions risk.

The case for a no-pay reputation rests on attackers favoring known payers. Industry surveys show that many payers are attacked again, but they do not report how often non-payers are, so the strength of this effect is unmeasured. It is the model’s most important assumption.

eta_b <- P$reputation$eta_base

## Why the policy must be credible

A no-pay policy is only worth what the crew believes about it, and a policy breaks under pressure when breaking it pays. In the moment, a fragile firm facing the first `r fmt_m(rep$opening, 2)` demand saves about `r fmt_m(fr$in_moment_saving)` by paying, because its walk-away point is `r fmt_m(fr$R_star, 2)`.

To test whether that temptation should win, we compare never paying with paying once, at the first incident, and never again. Costs are in present value at a `r fmt_pct(P$reputation$discount_rate)` discount rate, so the saving on the day counts in full and later attacks count for less. For the fragile firm, paying once costs `r fmt_m(pv_of(eta_b, "fragile", "deviate_once"))` over 10 years against `r fmt_m(pv_of(eta_b, "fragile", "never"))` for never paying: the extra attacks it invites outweigh the saving, so the policy is credible, by a margin of `r fmt_m(pv_of(eta_b, "fragile", "deviate_once") - pv_of(eta_b, "fragile", "never"))`. For our firm the margin is `r fmt_m(pv_of(eta_b, "typical", "deviate_once") - pv_of(eta_b, "typical", "never"))`. If crews ignore past payments, the result reverses: paying once saves the fragile firm `r fmt_m(pv_of(0, "fragile", "never") - pv_of(0, "fragile", "deviate_once"))`, and the policy is not credible.

A resilient firm faces no such test. Its walk-away point is `r fmt_m(prof$R_star[prof$profile == "resilient"], 2)`, so refusing is also its cheapest move on the day. Its policy is believed because it never has to be enforced against its own interest. Resilience is what turns a stated policy into a believed one.

The case for a no-pay reputation rests on attackers favoring known payers. Industry surveys show that many payers are attacked again, but they do not report how often non-payers are, so the strength of this effect is unmeasured. It is the model's most important assumption.
