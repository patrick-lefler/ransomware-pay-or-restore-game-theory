How to read the table. Each row is a Monte Carlo run of 200,000 simulated incidents, each with its own inputs and a random demand. The first row uses this firm’s ranges. The second shrinks restore times so that the median is 5 days and makes backups work about 90% of the time. The last column is the share of incidents in which paying is cheaper than restoring.

What the table tells you. With this firm’s ranges, paying is the cheaper path in 65% of incidents; with faster restores and stronger backups, in 9%. Reported market payment rates lie between the two runs, so the model brackets the market rather than matching it. The high first figure reflects this firm’s slow restores: the same model with market-like recovery pays far less often.

The simulated pay share is high because this firm restores slowly: its median restore time is 13.6 days, against roughly a week for most victims (Sophos 2025). At the base demand of $2.5M, paying on day one is cheaper in 64% of draws. The market payment rate was 23% in Q3 2025 (Coveware), fell to a new record low in Q2 2026 (Coveware), and nearly 80% of Q3 2026 incidents were resolved without payment (Coveware). That rate lies between the two runs, about 2.5 times the market-calibration figure. Observed payment rates also reflect insurance, law-enforcement involvement and corporate policy, which this model leaves out.

sbs |>

             T_r = "Restore time", b = "Backup credibility", D = "Ransom demand") |>
  tab_spanner("Share of variation in the pay decision", c(E_T, T_r, b, D)) |>
  fmt_number(columns = sdlog, decimals = 1) |>
  fmt_percent(columns = c(pay_share, E_T, T_r, b, D), decimals = 0) |>
  gt_board()

How to read the table. Each row reruns the 200,000 simulated incidents with demands spread more or less widely around the same median. The first column measures that spread: 0.6 is the site’s moderate assumption, and 1.4 is closer to the spread of published payments across all victims. The next column is how often paying is cheaper. The rest show how much of the yes-or-no decision each input explains on its own.

What the table tells you. The ranking flips as demands vary more widely. At the moderate spread, expected outage time explains 39% of the decision and the demand 15%; at the widest, the demand explains 43%. What holds at every spread is structural: restore time sets the walk-away point, and the demand only tests it. The pay share also falls as demands spread out, because more of them land far above the walk-away point.

Limitations

  • Synthetic firm. Every cost and probability is illustrative; a real decision needs the firm’s own outage cost, restore-test results and leak exposure.
  • Posted demand. The crew names one price. Negotiated discounts exist, so treating the demand as final overstates the cost of paying.
  • No cyber insurance. Insurance would cover part of the outage and possibly the ransom, which shifts the walk-away point.
  • One crew. The model has a single crew that learns from our history. Several crews, or crews that share intelligence, would change how fast reputation builds.
  • Leak cost is fixed. It does not depend on the number or sensitivity of the records taken.
  • The targeting response is uncertain. The reputation result holds only if crews favor known payers: above about 0.24 for our firm and 0.59 for the fragile firm; see the Decision Map & Reputation page.
  • The crew learns about our walk-away point, not our policy. A firm that never pays looks like one with a low walk-away point, so its demand keeps falling instead of the crew moving on.
  • The staged path needs time. Restoring first and paying only if backups fail assumes the firm can negotiate while restoring; leak deadlines or rising demands can remove that option.
  • Sanctions are a cost, not a bar. A known sanctions link would rule out payment entirely rather than add a penalty.
  • Outage cost is constant per day. In practice client churn accelerates after the first week, which would make restore time matter even more.
  • The decryptor works fully or not at all. Partial decryption would leave some restore effort even after paying.

What the table tells you. 12 of 12 checks pass. The formula matches an event-by-event simulation, the interactive map uses exactly the same formula as the R code, the simulation results move smoothly as the attack rate changes, and the main ranking of drivers does not depend on the random seed. Together they show that the numbers follow from the stated assumptions; they cannot show that the assumptions are right.
